Tibial plateau fractures. The Schatzker classification describes six types. Type I is a pure lateral split, typical in younger patients with dense cancellous bone. Type II is a lateral split-depression, the most common type. Type III is pure central depression of the lateral plateau, typical in osteoporotic bone. Type IV involves the medial plateau and is associated with high-energy injury, knee dislocation equivalents and risk to the popliteal artery and peroneal nerve. Type V is bicondylar. Type VI has metaphyseal-diaphyseal dissociation. Compartment syndrome is more frequent in types V and VI. Staged management with spanning external fixation is used for high-energy injuries with soft tissue compromise; definitive fixation is delayed until the skin wrinkle sign returns, usually 10-21 days. The posteromedial fragment is best addressed through a posteromedial approach (Lobenhoffer). Lateral meniscus tears are common with type II injuries. Articular step-off over 2 mm or condylar widening over 5 mm, and varus or valgus instability over 10 degrees in extension, are commonly cited indications for surgery. CT scan is the investigation of choice for preoperative planning of articular depression.
Scaphoid fractures. The scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal bone. The blood supply enters distally through the dorsal carpal branch of the radial artery, so proximal pole fractures have the highest risk of avascular necrosis. MRI is the investigation of choice for suspected occult scaphoid fracture when radiographs are normal. The Herbert classification divides fractures into stable type A and unstable type B. Displacement over 1 mm is an indication for internal fixation. Scaphoid nonunion advanced collapse (SNAC) wrist begins with arthritis at the radial styloid.
